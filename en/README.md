# Designing a Product from Scratch

This booklet explains how to design products with a user-centered approach.

## Scope of this booklet

The products this booklet talks about are mainly software, though parts of it apply to hardware products as well. Also, "design" here means design in the sense of **planning how a product works**. It does not cover aesthetic design. Some people call this process product planning, but I personally think "design" is the better word. Planning is only one part of the design process.

There are many ways to design a product, and this booklet covers **user-centered** product design. The term "user-centered" may not mean much at first. User-centered means deeply understanding users and the situations they are in, and making design decisions based on that understanding and on users' evaluations. So it is different from simply following users' opinions or building whatever users say they want. An example of an approach that is not user-centered is craftsmanship-driven design, based on intuition and reflection. I think that is a perfectly valid approach, but it is a very different kind of approach, so I don't cover it here.

User-centered design does not guarantee commercial success. Many factors affect commercial success. Timing matters, and so do marketing and operations. These things are important, but they have little to do with product design, so I don't cover them. In short, user-centered design is a way to make good products, and a good product does not automatically lead to commercial success.

Besides design, this booklet also covers how to communicate the results once you have designed something. In my experience, expressing your thoughts in writing or in a presentation is quite hard. I used to wish someone would explain how to do it, so I've included the things I wish I had known earlier.

Implementation matters as much as design. A design that never gets built is meaningless, I think. But I don't talk about implementation here. Perhaps I'll write about it somewhere else.

Much of this booklet is the author's opinion. Sentences stated firmly may read like facts rather than opinions, but anything without a reference is the author's opinion. Where there is a reference, I've cited it.

The booklet uses the term `design team`, but it does not mean only designers. It means the team that designs the product, which can include developers, designers, marketers, and other experts. The more varied the people on it, the better.

## Who is this booklet for?

I wrote this booklet for people who have little experience designing products at a company. I think it can help:

- Students or designers who want to work as UX designers
- Developers who want to design services, not just build them
- Anyone who wants to build products in a user-centered way

### About the structure

The chapters are arranged so that following them in order takes you through designing one product. I borrowed this structure from the book *Sprint* by Jake Knapp, John Zeratsky, and Braden Kowitz. In fact, I drew on that book a lot when writing this booklet.

That said, if you actually follow this process, you'll find that it doesn't move in a straight line. After doing the third step, you may feel you need to go back to the first. When that happens, it's good to go back and then come forward again. The design process is hard precisely because it is not linear. Design teams usually want work to move step by step. The gap between what we want and what actually happens makes us anxious and frustrated, but understanding that design is a nonlinear process by nature can ease that anxiety a little.

## How this differs from other writing on product design

There is a huge amount of material on product design and UX research, covering a vast range. But I felt there was little for people building commercial products without much experience. Existing material tends to discuss design in general (*The Design of Everyday Things* by Don Norman, *Good Services* by Lou Downe), focus on research (*User Research* by Stephanie Marsh), concentrate on a particular method ('[고작 다섯 명이 한 말을 어떻게 믿어요?](https://product.kyobobook.co.kr/detail/S000214928147)' by Rayoung Song, in Korean; *Trustworthy Online Controlled Experiments: A Practical Guide to A/B Testing* by Ron Kohavi, Diane Tang, and Ya Xu), or present methods that call for a lot of experience (*Sprint* by Jake Knapp, John Zeratsky, and Braden Kowitz). I wanted something you could read and act on right away. The closest book I know of is *Lean UX* by Jeff Gothelf and Josh Seiden. But I found it hard to run a project by following that book in order. I thought there was a need for a booklet that is simpler than *Lean UX* and that you can follow step by step to run a project.

This booklet does not go into design techniques in detail. Much of it is big-picture, and when it does describe specific techniques, it describes versions the author has simplified. That is why it is very short and simple. I hope it can serve as a stepping stone into the deeper world of design. Where more depth is needed, I don't include it here but point to other material where you can find it. All of the books mentioned above are good books. They are more specific, more precise, and more thorough, so if you want to learn more about product design, I recommend reading them.

Anyone can email the author about what this booklet is missing. I plan to keep updating it based on a variety of input.
