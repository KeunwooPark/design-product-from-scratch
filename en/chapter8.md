# Chapter 8: Writing a Design Report

Communicating well through documents is very important. It's a way to communicate efficiently with more people in the organization, and also with people in other times and places, including your future self.

What matters in a design report isn't only the result. It's important that the report covers the whole process of how the final result came about. Through it, others learn not only about the product but also about the users. That can help a lot when improving the product later, and also when building other products for similar target users. In terms of the design process, too, there will be many parts that can be applied to other projects. That's why explaining the intermediate results and the process matters as much as the final result.

There is no set format for a report, but the structure of a research paper is a good frame to refer to. The following structure is one example:

1. Introduction: Explain the problem the product aims to solve and its background, and briefly describe the final product.
2. Domain knowledge: Explain the domain knowledge researched in [Chapter 1](chapter1.md). This is for readers who don't have the domain knowledge for this project. Papers usually call this _related work_.
3. Product description: Explain **in detail** what the product is, what it looks like, and how it works.
4. Design process: Explain the important parts of each stage carried out earlier. Describe the process by which you arrived at the solution, and how the usability tests were run and what they showed. Writing everything down could be a lot, so include a reasonable level of detail.
5. Discussion and future plans: Describe any points for discussion, and talk about where the product should go next.

Keep the following in mind when writing a report:

- **Decide on the reader first.** A report for the development team and a report for executives emphasize different things. If it's hard to decide on a reader, picture a colleague six months from now who knows nothing about this project.
- **Write the conclusion first.** The introduction should contain all the important points, including the results. Not many people read a report to the end. Also, put the most important sentence at the start of each paragraph, so that reading just the first sentence of each paragraph is enough to understand it.
- **Attach reasons to decisions.** Don't stop at "We added a note on the order-complete screen asking customers to wait at a seat inside"; also write the reason: "because we observed customers waiting for drinks blocking the entrance." This is where the insights derived earlier and the usability test results come in.
- **Record what you discarded, too.** Keep the HMW questions that weren't chosen and the sketches that were dropped, along with why they weren't chosen. This keeps you from going through the same deliberation again later, and gives you a starting point when improving the product.
- **Make good use of visuals.** Visuals are very important. They might include the UX blueprint sketch, affinity diagrams, storyboards, and photos of prototypes. A single picture is far more effective than a long written explanation.

## Presenting

Once you've written the report, making a presentation isn't hard, because a presentation is a summary of the report. But unlike readers of a report, listeners can't go back and look again. So rather than trying to include everything, keep only the main flow: the problem you're solving, how you solved it, and the evidence. Put only one message on each slide, and point anyone who wants the details to the report.

Structure the presentation with the conclusion first, too. Explain first what you ultimately built. Then go on to explain why you built this product and what the results were; that order works well. You may want to save the great product you built for the very end, but that only bores your listeners.
